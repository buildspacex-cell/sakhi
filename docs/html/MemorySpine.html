Journal

We start with evidence, not interpretation.

Every journal entry is stored verbatim, once, and never rewritten. It is treated as a historical record of the user’s inner world at that moment — not a working note, not a draft, not a hypothesis.

Think of a trusted human companion listening to you. On day one, they may form a tentative opinion about what you’re going through. Weeks later, after hearing more context, they might realize they misunderstood parts of it — but they would never rewrite what you originally said. They refine their understanding, not your words.

Freezing interpretation too early destroys future insight and erodes trust. Sakhi allows understanding to evolve over time without ever altering what the user originally expressed.

All intelligence layers build on top of journals, but none overwrite them.

Recall

Human memory doesn’t work by replaying everything in order. It works by association.

Think of a friend saying, “This situation feels familiar,” and suddenly recalling a moment from years ago — not because they searched chronologically, but because something about the feeling, theme, or meaning matched.

Sakhi does the same. Each journal entry is transformed into a semantic representation that captures meaning rather than wording. This allows past moments to be recalled later when new situations resemble them — even if the words are completely different.

The original journal text is never altered. The embedding is simply a way for memory to recognize similarity, so context can be stitched together when it matters.

Short-Term Memory

When you’re talking to someone you trust, they don’t bring up your entire history every time you speak. They stay with what’s recent — what you just said, how you sounded, what seemed to matter a moment ago.

Short-term memory exists to hold the present moment together. It keeps Sakhi attuned to the emotional and conversational now, without overwhelming the interaction with everything from the past.

Think of it as the mental space a human uses to stay in a conversation: remembering the last few exchanges, the tone, the unresolved threads — not forever, just long enough to remain coherent and responsive.

This layer is intentionally small and temporary. It supports flow and presence now, while leaving deeper understanding to be formed later, more carefully.

Deep Recall

As time passes, short-term memory fades — but important moments shouldn’t disappear.

Think of how a person can no longer recall the details of every conversation, yet still remembers the moments that shaped them: turning points, recurring struggles, meaningful realizations.

Deep recall exists to preserve those moments in a durable, searchable form — not as raw transcripts, but as compact memory traces that can be revisited and reinterpreted later.

This layer does not replace short-term memory. It quietly accumulates beneath it, allowing long-term understanding to emerge without intruding on the present.

Conversation

When someone speaks, they are asking to be heard — not to be analyzed, categorized, or updated.

Sakhi treats each live turn as a moment of presence. The goal is to respond appropriately now, not to finalize understanding.

Think of a trusted friend in conversation. As you speak, they notice how you sound, what themes are emerging, and what you might be reaching toward — but they don’t stop you mid‑sentence to update their opinion of who you are. They stay with you.

During a turn, Sakhi notices lightweight signals that help it stay grounded in the moment, without committing to conclusions.

Conversation — Behavior Profile

Even with the same words, how someone responds matters.

Think of a close friend versus a colleague hearing the same concern. One might respond gently and quietly, the other more directly — not because the situation changed, but because the relationship and moment are different.

Sakhi forms a behavior profile for each turn to decide how to respond — not what to conclude.

This profile is built primarily from the current journal and turn signals (emotion, topics, intent), and is softly shaped by what Sakhi already knows: relationship state, persona traits, and past interaction style. Nothing new is learned or committed here.

Conversation — Brain Snapshot

When you speak with someone who knows you well, they don’t have to consciously recall everything about you. They already carry a sense of where you are in life, what you care about, and what you’re dealing with right now.

Sakhi does something similar. It keeps a brain snapshot — a unified, read‑only picture of the user’s current state — so it can respond with context without re‑figuring everything from scratch.

This snapshot is not built during the conversation. It is assembled quietly in the background from memory, rhythm, identity, relationship, and goals, and then reused during live turns.

Conversation — Day Matters

When you talk to someone at the end of a long day, you instinctively respond differently than you would in the morning. The words might be the same — but timing, energy, and capacity change everything.

Sakhi accounts for this by carrying a sense of where the day is for the user — not just who they are.

This context is prepared before the conversation. Daily and micro cues are computed ahead of time so the live turn can stay fast, grounded, and emotionally appropriate.

Personal Intelligence — Rhythm

Humans are not consistent machines. Energy, focus, and emotional capacity rise and fall — across a day, across a week, and across longer biological cycles.

Think of how the same task can feel effortless one day and overwhelming another. A thoughtful companion doesn’t assume motivation has changed — they recognize rhythm.

Sakhi maintains a living rhythm model to stay attuned to when support is helpful, not just what to say or do.

This rhythm understanding is built gradually in the background, from repeated signals over time. During conversation, it is only read — never inferred on the spot.

Personal Intelligence — The Memory Spine

Human understanding doesn’t grow as a pile of separate memories. It grows as a spine — a structure that holds experiences together, lets meaning accumulate, and allows reinterpretation without contradiction.

Think of how your sense of a person or a phase of life evolves. New experiences don’t erase old ones; they settle into place, reshaping how earlier moments are understood while leaving the moments themselves intact.

Sakhi’s memory spine serves the same role. It is the organizing structure that keeps all intelligence layers coherent over time, even as they learn at different speeds.

Personal Intelligence — Async by Design

Human understanding doesn’t happen all at once.

After an important conversation, if someone pauses too long to respond — visibly sorting, categorizing, or rethinking everything you said — it feels unsettling. Humans expect presence first. Deeper processing happens later, quietly, while life continues.

Sakhi is built the same way. Conversation stays fast and present. Learning happens later, when there is time, distance, and enough evidence to be careful.

Personal Intelligence — Action Planning

In human conversation, unsolicited plans can feel intrusive.

If someone immediately turns your reflection into a to‑do list, it can feel like they’ve taken control away — even if their intentions are good. Thoughtful humans sense when it’s time to suggest, and when it’s time to wait.

Sakhi follows this same principle. During conversation, it may recognize potential actions and gently surface them as possibilities — not commitments.

Actual planning happens later, in the background, once there is enough clarity, repetition, and user intent to justify structure.

Meta-Reflection

Sometimes understanding doesn’t come from a single moment, but from stepping back and looking at a stretch of experience as a whole.

Think of a thoughtful human who, days or weeks after an event, says something like: “I’ve been thinking about what you shared earlier — there’s a pattern here.” That reflection wouldn’t have felt right in the moment. It needed distance.

Meta-reflection is Sakhi’s way of creating that distance-aware understanding.

After conversations and reflections accumulate, Sakhi quietly gathers higher-order signals in the background:

  • Recurring themes across journals and reflections
  • Shifts in emotional tone over time
  • Patterns in energy, rhythm, and stress
  • Alignment or tension between stated values and lived behavior
  • Changes in how reflections land with the user (feedback signals)

These signals are never pushed immediately. They are queued, synthesized, and only surfaced when timing and readiness are appropriate.

Identity Evolves Slowly

What: Sakhi maintains an evolving identity layer composed of values, life arcs, purpose themes, and conflicts.

Why: Identity inferred too quickly becomes inaccurate and unsafe; real identity emerges from repeated patterns.

How: Background workers periodically rebuild identity structures from accumulated journals, reflections, and themes.

Serves: Grounds long-term understanding of the person without freezing them into labels.

Persona Is Style

What: A set of persona traits that define how Sakhi speaks and shows up.

Why: Alignment is not just about content, but about tone, warmth, and expressiveness.

How: Persona traits are inferred from conversational patterns and blended gradually over time.

Serves: Allows Sakhi’s voice to adapt without destabilizing trust or consistency.

Relationship State

What: An internal model of trust, attunement, and emotional safety.

Why: The same guidance can feel supportive or intrusive depending on relationship context.

How: Relationship state is updated from tone, sentiment, and user pushback signals.

Serves: Shapes how and when Sakhi engages, protecting psychological safety.

Why Background Matters

What: Identity, persona, and relationship updates all occur off the live turn path.

Why: Errors in these layers are costly and difficult to undo.

How: Slow, periodic rebuilds ensure stability and allow corrections.

Serves: Prevents abrupt shifts and preserves user trust over time.

Insight Generation

What: The process of turning accumulated state into reflective insights.

Why: Users benefit from patterns and alignment more than raw data.

How: Insight engines read brain state, summaries, and narratives to produce insight bundles.

Serves: Provides meaning-oriented feedback without prescribing action.

Presence Follow-Ups

What: Scheduled prompts and messages that maintain continuity.

Why: Growth and reflection happen between conversations, not only during them.

How: Presence workers generate tone-matched follow-ups based on past reflections.

Serves: Keeps Sakhi present without being reactive or intrusive.

Learning From Feedback

What: A feedback loop that evaluates how reflections land with the user.

Why: Reflection quality improves only if the system learns what resonates.

How: User responses are classified and used to recalibrate reflection style and depth.

Serves: Improves reflection relevance and trustworthiness over time.

Memory Graph

What: A latent graph connecting themes, emotions, rhythm signals, and reflections.

Why: Human understanding is relational, not flat.

How: Background workers periodically infer nodes and edges from accumulated signals.

Serves: Prepares the system for richer recall and reasoning without depending on it prematurely.

Consolidation

What: Periodic aggregation jobs that turn repeated signals into durable structure.

Why: Raw journals and signals lose meaning unless recurring patterns are promoted and stabilized.

How: Scheduled workers scan recent history to promote frequent tags into themes, activate repeatedly mentioned goals, and aggregate the last 7 days into a short-horizon snapshot.

Serves: Ensures the system recognizes what is becoming important without hard-coding conclusions too early.

Maintenance

What: Ongoing background upkeep of caches, analytics, tempo, and derived state.

Why: A living system decays without care; stale caches and misaligned state lead to poor guidance.

How: Scheduled workers refresh planner caches, analytics summaries, task-weaver state, growth sync, and system tempo metrics.

Serves: Keeps the system fast, coherent, and trustworthy without recomputation during live turns.

Why Maintenance Matters

What: The invisible work that keeps Sakhi accurate over weeks and months.

Why: Without consolidation and maintenance, intelligence drifts, recommendations misalign, and trust erodes.

How: By continuously reconciling recent behavior with long-term structure and refreshing derived views.

Serves: Allows Sakhi to age gracefully — improving with time instead of accumulating errors.

The Architectural Reveal

What: Sakhi operates on two clearly separated execution planes.

Why: Presence and learning have fundamentally different requirements and failure modes.

How: Every capability naturally falls into either the Live Turn Plane or the Worker Plane.

Serves: Makes the entire system explainable, debuggable, and safe at scale.

What: The real-time conversational experience the user interacts with.

Why: Users need immediacy, empathy, and coherence in the moment.

Why This Architecture Ages Well

What: A system designed to improve without becoming rigid.

Why: Most AI systems fail by hardening early assumptions.

Serves: Ensures Sakhi becomes more accurate, humane, and trustworthy over time.
